Guide · Self-reviews

Self-review examples for SRE and DevOps engineers.

If you did the job well, the year looked uneventful from outside. Fewer pages, faster deploys, incidents that ended in minutes. A good SRE self-review turns that calm into a record: the load you took off other people, and the risks you closed before they became incidents.

What reviewers look for in SRE and DevOps

  • Reliability against a target. SLOs and error budgets, with the numbers.
  • Less toil. Manual work you automated away, measured in hours or tickets.
  • Safer change. Deploy frequency, lead time, change failure rate and time to roll back.
  • Incident follow-through. Blameless reviews, and how many action items actually closed.
  • Other teams moving faster. Self-serve environments, templates and docs that cut the tickets sent to you.

Example: impact

The projects, people and numbers in these examples are made up. Keep the shape and use your own facts.

Impact • Defined SLOs for the Sync SDK API (99.9% of sync requests succeed over 28 days) with error-budget alerts. Pages for the team dropped from about 30 a week to 8. • Moved deploys from a weekly manual window to on-merge pipelines with a canary stage and automatic rollback. A deploy now takes about 12 minutes instead of 45. • Made staging environment access self-serve with short-lived credentials. Requests that used to wait days now take minutes, and the shared staging password is gone.

The best SRE numbers are about people: pages per week, hours of waiting, tickets that no longer get filed.

Example: collaboration

Collaboration • Facilitated five incident reviews, all blameless, each with owners and dates. 14 of 17 action items are closed; the other three are scheduled. • Paired with the Recipe App 2.0 team on their first dashboards and alerts before launch, so their own service pages them. • Wrote the new-service template with Neha. New services start with logging, metrics and a runbook stub.

Example: growth

Growth • Learned to design reusable Terraform modules instead of copying folders. Three teams use the network module now. • Joined the incident commander rota and asked for feedback after each incident. My status updates got shorter and more regular. • Next half: monthly capacity and spend reviews for the sync cluster.

Phrases that land

  • "99.9% of sync requests over 28 days": a target anyone can check against the graph.
  • "pages from about 30 a week to 8": the human cost, reduced.
  • "requests that waited days now take minutes": toil measured by the people who felt it.
  • "14 of 17 action items closed": follow-through, with the gap admitted.

Phrases that don't

  • "Kept the lights on." Everyone hopes so. What did you change?
  • "Lots of firefighting." It sounds heroic and reads as a system nobody fixed.
  • "Managed the infrastructure." Which parts, and what got better?
  • "Was on call." Say what on-call taught you and what you changed after it.

Gather your evidence

With infrastructure as code, your changes are in git too. This lists your commits to infrastructure folders:

git log --since="6 months ago" --author="$(git config user.email)" --no-merges \ --date=short --format='%ad %s' -- terraform k8s .github/workflows

This finds commits that mention incidents, rollbacks or reverts, by anyone. It's a quick index into the hard weeks:

git log --all --since="6 months ago" -i --grep='incident' --grep='rollback' \ --grep='revert' --date=short --format='%ad %an %s'

Then export the pager history (pages per week, before and after your changes), the deploy pipeline's run history, and links to each incident review you ran or joined.
